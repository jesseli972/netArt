Curatorial Statement

In October 2009, Yahoo shut down GeoCities and deleted roughly 38 million personal homepages overnight. Years of self-expression, guestbooks, blinking GIFs, tiled backgrounds, and "under construction" banners just disappeared. This exhibition brings together three projects that each try to do something with what was left behind, using the same archive of rescued GeoCities pages but approaching it in very different ways.

Richard Vijgen's The Deleted City (2011) turns the 650-gigabyte backup into an interactive map you can zoom through, treating the archive like a city you can explore. Cameron Askin's Cameron's World (2015) pulls images, GIFs, and text from thousands of archived pages and stitches them into a wild scrolling collage. And Olia Lialina and Dragan Espenschied's One Terabyte of Kilobyte Age (2010–ongoing) takes a slower approach, restoring and sharing individual homepage screenshots one at a time.

What connects these works is that they all respond to the same event — the deletion of GeoCities — but each one preserves it differently. One maps it, one remixes it, and one archives it page by page. Together, they show that there is no single way to remember a lost online community, and that the act of preservation is itself a creative decision. Each project reflects not just what GeoCities was, but what the artists think is worth saving about it.

Works in the Exhibition
01 / 03

The Deleted City

Richard Vijgen · 2011

An interactive visualization of the 650-gigabyte GeoCities backup made by the Archive Team in 2009. The project maps the entire file system like a city, arranging GeoCities' themed neighborhoods by size. As you zoom in, individual HTML pages and their images become visible. It has been exhibited at the Los Angeles County Museum of Art, the Barbican, and the Computer History Museum.

02 / 03

Cameron's World

Cameron Askin · 2015

A sprawling web collage made from text, images, and GIF animations pulled from archived GeoCities pages. The site is organized into thematic rows with over 700 images, all sourced from pages created between 1994 and 2009. Described by its creator as a tribute to "the lost days of unrefined self-expression on the Internet," it captures the chaotic, personal energy of early homepages.

View the work →
03 / 03

One Terabyte of Kilobyte Age

Olia Lialina & Dragan Espenschied · 2010–ongoing

An ongoing archival project that restores and shares screenshots of nearly 400,000 GeoCities homepages saved before Yahoo deleted the platform in 2009. Unlike the other works in this show, it presents each page individually, giving every homepage its own moment rather than folding it into a larger visualization or collage.

Thematic Connections
01 — Same Archive, Different Approaches

All three projects draw from the same source: the GeoCities archive rescued by the Archive Team and Internet Archive before Yahoo's 2009 shutdown. But each one does something different with that material. Vijgen maps it spatially, Askin collages it visually, and Lialina and Espenschied present it one page at a time. The variety shows that archiving is never neutral — how you choose to present saved material shapes what people take away from it.

02 — Preservation as Creative Practice

None of these works simply store old files. Each one makes active decisions about what to highlight, how to organize it, and what story to tell about the early web. The Deleted City treats GeoCities like a lost civilization to explore. Cameron's World celebrates the messy, personal creativity of 90s homepages. One Terabyte of Kilobyte Age treats each page as something worth looking at on its own. Preservation, in all three cases, is an artistic act.

03 — What Gets Lost When Platforms Disappear

Together, these works make a case that when a platform like GeoCities gets deleted, we don't just lose files. We lose the weird, personal, unpolished way people used to express themselves online before social media gave everyone the same profile template. All three projects push back against that loss by showing what early web self-expression actually looked and felt like.
